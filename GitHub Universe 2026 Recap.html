A NOTE BEFORE THE EXCITING PARTS

Just because you can build it doesn't mean you should.

The industry as a whole is encouraging people to hand more work to AI. That's an opportunity, and it comes with responsibility. It's on us to do our due diligence:

–Stay sharp. Don't hand off every piece of work to agents. Keep thinking critically so we don't get lazy in our work.
–Work with purpose and intent. Being deliberate about what we hand off also helps us avoid AI burnout.
–Justify the spend. The goal isn't to max out tokens. Ask whether a piece of work is worth the AI credits before spending them.
–Tie it to our goals. Understand the problem and direct the work, and make sure it falls within our requirements and company goals.

03 · YOUR ROLE

Developers were the starting point, not the finish line.

This wasn't only my read. GitHub's opening keynote described where the industry is heading: an SDLC that's less of a straight line from planning to coding to testing to deployment, where work can start from a code change, a colleague's request, or a production metric. Our process works the way it does for good reasons, so this isn't a call to change it overnight. It's a direction worth watching. We'll see how it affects our software teams over time, and we'll look to leadership to set how we adopt it.

“Completed work, not more code, is the output that matters.”

Jay Parikh, EVP of CoreAI at Microsoft, opening keynote

Design

Developers

Managers

FROM GITHUB NEXT · MULTIPLAYER MODE

Chopin: planning as a team sport

In the closing keynote, GitHub Next showed Chopin, an experimental prototype for collaborative planning with agents. The problem it targets: planning with AI today is a private, one-on-one chat, and teammates often don't see a plan until the pull request, which is too late to ask "should we even build this?"

•A shared planning canvas connected to the repository, open to the whole team.
•The agent follows the discussion, researches trade-offs, and draws diagrams as people talk.
•Clickable prototypes to react to before any production code is written.
•Decision cards that record who chose what, and when.

This is the product development collaboration I'd love to see us get to: product, BAs, design, QA, and developers planning together, with agents helping and people owning the decisions.

The same thinking applies to reuse. Rather than everyone rediscovering the same AI-assisted process, successful skills, prompts, and workflows should be created, shared, discovered, reused, improved, and trusted, which is where our AI Exchange work fits. Keep an eye out for an announcement soon. I'm looking forward to seeing people contribute to it.

05 · WHAT ABOUT MY JOB?

If AI handles the busy work, what's left for us?

It's the question underneath all of this, and both keynotes took it on directly. The opening keynote described how quickly the job has moved, from writing every line of code, to working with AI assistants, to orchestrating fleets of agents, and said that developers are more important than ever. The closing keynote added that shipping faster only helps if we're building the right things. People still have to understand the problem, direct the work, and judge whether the result is any good.

Writing every line→Planning & architecting

More of our time goes into defining the problem, the constraints, and what good looks like before work starts. Sessions like "Humans set direction. Agents close the loop" were built around this.

Doing the busy work→Creative, collaborative work

AI picks up the repetitive tasks so people can spend more time with each other, with users, and on the harder problems.

Handing off tasks→Orchestrating the work

You can delegate a lot, but someone still steers it toward the bigger vision and stays accountable for what ships.

Knowing a fixed toolset→Learning continuously

The tools will keep changing. Staying familiar with them is part of the job now. The closing keynote's advice for feeling out of date was simple: learn by doing.

Martin Woodward during the closing keynote.
 

“In this new era, AI doesn't write the story. You write the story.”

Martin Woodward, GitHub, closing keynote

That matches what a lot of us here have already been saying. The tools are changing, and it's on each of us to get familiar with them, so we spend less time on the boring parts and more on the work that needs people.

IF IT ALL CAME DOWN TO ONE IDEA

The first phase of AI was about helping developers code faster. The next phase is about improving the whole delivery system around them.

What I heard from much larger organizations lined up closely with what we've been exploring: shared skills, common training, trusted agents, and repository-backed collaboration. It was good validation that we're heading in the right direction.
